# Literature Synthesis Patterns

Use this reference when extracted literature material must be logically combined instead of directly mapped to an outline.

## Material Functions

Classify extracted material by function:

- Background: explains why the phenomenon matters.
- Concept definition: defines or differentiates constructs.
- Theoretical premise: states assumptions from a theory.
- Mechanism: explains why or how a relationship occurs.
- Empirical precedent: reports prior findings.
- Debate or tension: reveals disagreement, inconsistency, or limitation.
- Gap: shows what prior work has not addressed.
- Boundary condition: limits when a claim applies.
- Method pattern: offers a reusable research design or measure.
- Hypothesis pattern: shows how a paper moves from theory to a formal hypothesis.

## Synthesis Moves

Use these moves to build paragraphs and sections:

- Convergence: multiple papers support a shared claim.
- Extension: one stream can be extended to a new context, construct, population, or mechanism.
- Contrast: papers make different assumptions, use different contexts, or reach different conclusions.
- Tension: prior findings or theories create an unresolved problem.
- Sequencing: one idea creates the premise for the next.
- Mechanism bridging: connect a theory to a variable relationship through explanatory steps.
- Gap narrowing: move from broad literature to the specific missing link the article addresses.

## Literature Review Logic

Do not list papers one by one. Build a section around a claim:

1. State the synthesis claim.
2. Define the concept or literature stream.
3. Summarize convergent evidence.
4. Contrast alternative views or limitations.
5. Explain what this means for the target article.

## Hypothesis Development Logic

Use this pattern:

1. Begin with a construct or relationship to be explained.
2. Introduce the theoretical premise.
3. Explain the mechanism in several steps.
4. Add supporting literature for each step.
5. Acknowledge boundary conditions or competing explanations when needed.
6. State the hypothesis.

## Safe Inference

Label the difference between source claims and author reasoning:

- "Source claim": directly supported by a cited paper.
- "Synthesis": combines multiple source claims.
- "Inference": a logical step made by the target article.
- "Speculation": a possible idea that needs more evidence.

Do not present inference or speculation as if a source explicitly made the claim.
