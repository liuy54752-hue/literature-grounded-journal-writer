# Journal Paper Literature Review Section

Use this reference only for the Literature Review or Theoretical Background section within a journal paper. Do not apply it as a protocol for a standalone review article, systematic review, meta-analysis, or PRISMA workflow.

## Method Basis

This workflow adapts relevant guidance from:

- Chan, W. C. W. (2023), "Writing Excellent Review Articles," *ACS Nano*.
- Fernandez, K. V. (2019), "Critically reviewing literature: A tutorial for new researchers," *Australasian Marketing Journal*.
- Dhillon, P. (2022), "How to write a good scientific review article," *The FEBS Journal*.

Apply only the principles relevant to a journal paper's Literature Review section: question-led scope, broad and current reading, source organization, critical assessment, thematic synthesis, accessible explanation, coherent narrative, and forward movement toward the target study. Do not copy source wording or import standalone-review requirements such as a review abstract, review-level conclusion, fixed reference count, or display-item quota.

## 1. Define the Review Task

Record before reading or drafting:

- The target paper's research question, proposed relationship, or theoretical problem.
- The function of this Literature Review: define concepts, reconcile streams, establish a mechanism, explain a debate, identify a boundary condition, or motivate hypotheses/research questions.
- The intended audience, discipline, target journal, language, and approximate section length.
- The included concepts, streams, contexts, periods, and source types.
- Explicit exclusions that prevent scope drift.
- The section's central message in one provisional sentence.

Use the research question to decide relevance. Do not aim to mention everything written about the broad topic.

## 2. Assemble the Literature

### Start with user-provided sources

1. Inventory all uploaded papers and available metadata.
2. Identify seminal, directly relevant, recent, contradictory, and methodologically informative sources.
3. Distinguish papers read in full from abstract-only records and references merely cited by another paper.
4. Follow backward references and forward citations conceptually to identify missing original work, but do not treat a citation lead as verified evidence.

### Diagnose coverage before searching

Report concrete gaps such as:

- A construct lacks an authoritative definition.
- A central variable relationship rests on one source.
- Only one theoretical perspective or research context is represented.
- A claimed contradiction lacks the original studies.
- Recent developments are absent.

Ask the user before external retrieval. If approved, record the search tools or databases, exact queries, search date, filters, and sources added. Match search syntax to the selected database. Prefer original articles over another review's paraphrase of them.

Do not claim that the search is exhaustive or systematic unless a separate, reproducible design actually establishes that fact.

## 3. Arrange the Literature

Build the Literature Review Evidence Matrix in `evidence-matrix-template.md`. Classify material by its role in the target argument rather than by reading order.

Possible organizing dimensions:

- Core concept or definition.
- Research stream or theoretical lens.
- Explanatory mechanism.
- Variable relationship.
- Empirical context or level of analysis.
- Consensus, contradiction, or unresolved debate.
- Boundary condition or methodological limitation.

Cluster sources that address the same claim. Chronology is useful when ideas genuinely develop over time, but it is not a default structure. Reorganize the matrix as themes emerge; Assemble, Arrange, and Assess are iterative rather than strictly linear.

## 4. Assess the Literature

For each important claim or stream, evaluate:

- Whether constructs are defined consistently.
- Whether theories make compatible assumptions.
- Whether methods and measures support the stated conclusions.
- Whether findings converge across contexts, samples, periods, and levels of analysis.
- Whether disagreement reflects theory, measurement, design, context, or interpretation.
- Which conclusions are robust, conditional, tentative, or unsupported.
- Which populations, settings, mechanisms, or relationships remain underexplained.

Be candid but evidence-based. Critical review does not mean fault-finding; it means explaining what the evidence can and cannot establish.

## 5. Map the Section Argument

Create a Literature Review Subsection Argument Map before drafting. Give each subsection one controlling claim and a clear function in the overall reasoning.

Prefer structures such as:

- Concept definition -> competing interpretations -> adopted definition.
- Stream A -> Stream B -> unresolved tension -> integration.
- Established relationship -> proposed mechanisms -> missing mechanism or boundary.
- Convergent evidence -> contradictory evidence -> explanation of divergence.
- Broad stream -> narrower unresolved link -> target-study positioning.

A defensible gap must follow from reviewed evidence. Specify what is missing, why it matters, and how the target study addresses it. Avoid novelty claims based only on phrases such as "few studies" or "no research" unless the evidence supports them.

## 6. Draft Analytical Units

Use this default movement across one or more paragraphs:

1. Topic claim: state the analytical point, not the name of a paper.
2. Multi-source synthesis: combine relevant evidence and show convergence, progression, or contrast.
3. Critical comparison: explain differences in definitions, theories, methods, contexts, or findings.
4. Unresolved issue: state the bounded limitation, tension, or missing link established by the comparison.
5. Target-study link: show how the paper's question, model, or hypothesis responds.

Do not mechanically force all five moves into every paragraph. A paragraph may perform one move within a larger subsection argument, but every paragraph must advance the controlling claim.

Write for informed readers beyond the narrow specialty:

- Define necessary technical terms at first use.
- Keep one clear message per paragraph and subsection.
- Use transitions that explain relationships among research streams.
- Prefer synthesis and precise paraphrase to long quotation.
- Use an original conceptual table or figure only when it materially clarifies relationships; do not add display items merely to decorate the section.

## 7. Audit the Draft

### Evidence and citation

- Verify that every factual or source-attributed claim is supported.
- Prefer the original paper for a specific finding.
- Label cited-source evidence whose original has not been read.
- Separate verified source claims, cross-source synthesis, and author inference.
- Remove fabricated or unconfirmed authors, years, titles, pages, DOIs, results, and claims.

### Synthesis and critique

- Flag paragraphs dominated by one-paper-at-a-time summaries.
- Confirm that multiple sources are related rather than merely placed together.
- Check that critique addresses evidential limits, not author reputation.
- Test whether each claimed consensus, conflict, limitation, or gap follows from the matrix.

### Narrative and article fit

- Confirm that each subsection advances the Literature Review's central message.
- Remove broad background already handled in the Introduction.
- Ensure the ending creates a justified bridge to the research question, model, or hypotheses.
- Confirm that the Discussion can later return to the same streams without introducing the literature foundation for the first time.

## Failure Modes

Reject or revise drafts that:

- Read as a catalogue of paper summaries.
- Follow a generic formula unrelated to the target question.
- Cite reviews when the specific original study is available and needed.
- Equate citation frequency with evidential strength.
- Declare a research gap without showing the reviewed basis.
- Hide contradictory findings or methodological limitations.
- Present an author's inference as a source's explicit claim.
- Use secondary citations as if their original sources were directly verified.
- Expand into a standalone review article instead of serving the target journal paper.
